Walking Isn’t Enough: How to Maintain Strength and Muscle as You Age

Walking is one of the simplest and most valuable ways to stay active as you get older. But if your goal is to maintain strength, balance, and independence, walking may need some help from other forms of exercise.

Updated October 2026 • 8–10 minute read

Key takeaways

  • Walking is excellent for cardiovascular health and everyday activity.
  • Walking alone does not provide the same muscle-strengthening stimulus as resistance exercise.
  • Older adults can benefit from combining aerobic activity, strength training, and balance exercises.
  • Strength training does not necessarily mean lifting heavy weights. Resistance bands, bodyweight exercises, and appropriately chosen weights can all be useful.
  • Starting gradually and choosing exercises appropriate for your abilities is more important than trying to do everything at once.

For many people, walking becomes the exercise they rely on most as they get older. It is accessible, inexpensive, and easy to fit into everyday life.

And walking absolutely has value. Regular physical activity can support cardiovascular health, mobility, mood, sleep, and overall physical function.

But there is another part of healthy aging that deserves just as much attention: maintaining muscle and strength.

Strength affects much more than how much weight you can lift. It can influence everyday tasks such as getting up from a chair, climbing stairs, carrying groceries, and moving around safely.

That is why a well-rounded exercise routine after 60 generally includes more than walking alone.

Why does muscle matter as we get older?

Muscle mass and strength naturally change with age. If muscles are not regularly challenged, strength and physical function can gradually decline.

This does not mean that losing strength is unavoidable or that getting older automatically means becoming weak.

Regular resistance exercise can help older adults maintain and improve strength and physical function.

This matters because everyday independence often depends on physical abilities that we may take for granted.

Strength is not just about exercise. It is about having enough physical ability to keep doing the things that matter in everyday life.

So, is walking still worth doing?

Absolutely.

The point is not that walking is bad or that you should stop walking. Walking is an excellent way to accumulate regular physical activity, especially for people who enjoy it and can do it comfortably.

The important distinction is that different types of exercise train different physical abilities.

Walking

Supports aerobic activity, mobility, and everyday movement.

Strength

Challenges muscles and helps maintain physical strength.

Balance

Helps train the ability to control and stabilize the body.

The three pieces of a well-rounded routine

A practical exercise routine for older adults does not need to be complicated. Think about three broad categories.

1. Aerobic activity

This includes activities that increase your breathing and heart rate. Brisk walking, cycling, swimming, and similar activities can fit here.

Walking can therefore remain a major part of your routine.

2. Muscle-strengthening activity

Strength exercises provide resistance that makes your muscles work harder than they normally would during everyday movement.

Depending on your abilities, this might include resistance bands, bodyweight exercises, machines, or free weights.

You do not need to become a competitive weightlifter. The goal is to challenge your muscles safely and progressively.

3. Balance and stability

Balance is another important part of staying physically capable as you age.

Simple balance activities can be incorporated into a routine, depending on your abilities and safety needs.

If you have significant balance problems, exercises should be selected with appropriate professional guidance.

What could a simple weekly routine look like?

There is no single exercise schedule that works for everyone. Your health, fitness level, previous injuries, medications, and preferences all matter.

However, the following example shows how different activities can fit together:

Example week

Monday
Walking + gentle mobility
Tuesday
Strength exercises
Wednesday
Walking or another comfortable aerobic activity
Thursday
Strength + balance exercises
Friday
Walking
Saturday
Optional light activity or walking
Sunday
Rest or gentle movement

This is only an example, not a personalized exercise prescription. Your routine should be adjusted to your abilities and health needs.

Simple strength exercises to consider

Strength training can be much simpler than many people imagine.

Depending on your fitness level, examples may include:

  • Standing up and sitting down from a sturdy chair
  • Wall push-ups
  • Resistance-band exercises
  • Light dumbbell exercises
  • Step-ups using an appropriate and secure step
  • Exercises recommended by a physical therapist or qualified professional

The right starting point depends on your current strength and health. Someone who has been inactive for years should not feel pressured to begin with a demanding workout.

Start small and gradually increase the challenge

One of the biggest mistakes people can make is trying to progress too quickly.

If you are new to resistance training, start with an amount of resistance and volume that you can perform with good control.

Over time, the exercises can become gradually more challenging as your body adapts.

You do not need the hardest workout. You need a routine you can perform consistently and safely.

What about protein?

Exercise is only one part of maintaining muscle. Nutrition also matters.

Protein provides amino acids that the body uses to build and maintain tissues, including muscle.

Good food sources include foods such as eggs, fish, poultry, dairy products, beans, lentils, soy foods, nuts, and seeds.

Older adults who have difficulty eating enough protein or who have kidney disease or other medical conditions should discuss their nutritional needs with a healthcare professional rather than automatically starting supplements.

Don't forget recovery

More exercise is not automatically better.

Your body needs time to recover between challenging activities. Adequate sleep, nutritious food, hydration, and appropriate rest can all support an active lifestyle.

If exercise consistently causes unusual or persistent pain, excessive fatigue, dizziness, or other concerning symptoms, stop and seek appropriate medical advice.

When should you talk with a healthcare professional?

If you have a chronic medical condition, significant joint or muscle problems, a history of falls, chest symptoms, or you have been inactive for a long time, it can be useful to discuss your exercise plans with a healthcare professional.

A physical therapist or appropriately qualified exercise professional may also help you choose exercises that match your abilities.

Safety should come before intensity.

The bottom line

Walking remains one of the best simple ways to stay active as you age. You do not need to give it up.

But if your goal is to maintain strength, mobility, balance, and independence, it makes sense to look beyond walking alone.

A combination of aerobic activity, muscle-strengthening exercises, and balance work can address different aspects of physical fitness.

Most importantly, you do not have to transform your routine overnight. Start with what you can do safely, build gradually, and focus on consistency.

Healthy aging is not about doing everything perfectly. It is about continuing to give your body opportunities to move, strengthen, and stay capable.
